How to Build an Athlete Media Kit That Gets You Brand Deals (Step-by-Step)

By ME3 Digital·19 min read·Updated July 25, 2026

Featured Snippet Answer

An athlete media kit is a short, professional document (usually 1–3 pages or a slide deck) that shows a brand who you are, who your audience is, and what it’s like to work with you. Include your bio, sport and stats, audience size and engagement, content examples, past partnerships, deliverables, and contact info. Think of it as your athletic resume for brand deals.

Picture this. A local business owner just watched your highlight reel, loved your energy, and typed four words into a DM that could change your season: “Do you have a media kit?”

Right now, one of two things happens. Either you fumble around, send a screenshot of your follower count, and hope for the best. Or you calmly attach a clean, one-page document that answers every question that owner is silently asking — and you look like a professional who has done this a hundred times before.

That second athlete gets the deal. Not because they have more followers. Because they made it easy to say yes.

I want to build that second athlete with you today. Not a hype version of you — the real, credible, prepared version that brands actually trust. Because here’s the thing most NIL advice gets wrong: it teaches you to promote yourself louder. We’re going to do something better. We’re going to make you genuinely valuable and genuinely easy to work with.

Let’s do this together.

What Is an Athlete Media Kit (and Why Brands Ask for One)

Let’s start with a scene, not a definition.

A marketing manager at a nutrition company has $2,000 to spend this month on athlete partnerships. Her inbox has forty messages from athletes. She has maybe fifteen minutes. She’s not looking for the best athlete — she’s looking for the one who makes her decision the safest. The one who clearly shows what they offer, who their audience is, and that they won’t be a headache to manage.

Your media kit is how you become that safe, obvious choice.

A media kit (sometimes called a NIL media kit or a sports media kit) is a short, polished document — one to three pages, or a simple slide deck — that packages the most important facts about you into one place. It’s your athletic resume for brand deals. When a brand asks “do you have a media kit,” they’re really asking: “Can I trust you to be professional, and can you help me understand if you’re a fit?”

Pull quote: Your media kit isn’t a brag sheet. It’s a trust document.

Brands ask for one because it saves them time and lowers their risk. A kit tells them you take this seriously, that you’ve thought about your value, and that working with you will feel organized instead of chaotic. That single impression often matters more than a few thousand extra followers.

The Psychology: What a Brand Is Really Looking For

Before we build a single section, you need to understand what’s happening on the other side of the table. This is the part almost nobody teaches you, and it’s the part that changes everything.

When a brand evaluates you, they’re quietly scoring three things:

  1. Fit — Does your audience overlap with their customers? A vegan protein brand doesn’t need your follower count; it needs to know your followers are people who might buy protein.
  2. Reliability — If they pay you or send you product, will you actually deliver, on time, the way you promised? Flakiness is a brand’s biggest fear.
  3. Ease — Will working with you be smooth? Or will they have to chase you, re-explain the brief, and fix your disclosures?

Notice that only one of those three is about your numbers. The other two are about your professionalism. That’s the whole game.

ME3 Insight: Most athletes try to win the deal by being impressive. The ones who win it repeatedly do it by being reliable. Brands don’t rebook the flashiest athlete — they rebook the easiest one.

At ME3, we sit on the brand side of these decisions. When we help a company choose athletes for a campaign, the kit that wins isn’t usually the one with the biggest audience. It’s the one that clearly answers “who is this person, who do they reach, and can I trust them.” Build your kit to answer those three questions and you’re already ahead of most of your competition.

The Section-by-Section Anatomy of a Media Kit

Here’s your blueprint. We’ll walk through it together, section by section. You don’t need every fancy element — you need the right ones, done cleanly.

1. Header + Bio (Who You Are)

Top of the page: your name, your sport, your school, your class year, and your handles. Then a short bio — three to four sentences, written in third person, like a broadcaster introducing you.

Your bio should cover: what you play, one thing that makes you memorable, and what your content is about. Not your whole life story. A brand should be able to “get” you in ten seconds.

Example (generic): “Jordan is a Division II soccer midfielder and first-generation college student who shares training routines, game-day nutrition, and honest student-athlete life with a tight-knit local following.”

2. Sport, Stats & Story (Your Credibility)

A few relevant athletic highlights — position, key stats, awards, or a milestone. Keep it honest and skimmable. But don’t stop at numbers: add one line of story. The comeback from injury. The walk-on who earned a starting spot. Brands buy stories as much as stats, because stories are what your audience shares.

Pro Tip: One authentic sentence of story (“came back from an ACL tear to start every game senior year”) is worth more than a wall of statistics a brand can’t interpret.

3. Audience & Engagement (Who You Reach)

This is the heart of the kit. Include:

  • Total following across your main platforms
  • Engagement rate (more on why this matters in the next section)
  • Audience demographics — rough age range, top locations, gender split, and interests, if you can pull them from your platform’s built-in analytics

You don’t need to be huge here. You need to be clear and honest. A brand can work with “1,800 followers, mostly 16–24, based in the Tampa area, high engagement.” A brand cannot work with a vague screenshot.

4. Content Style & Examples (What You Actually Make)

Show, don’t tell. Add two or three thumbnail images of your best posts — a Reel that popped, a clean training photo, a story series. If it’s a digital kit, link them. This section answers the brand’s quiet question: “What will my product look like in this person’s hands?”

Describe your style in a phrase or two: “bright, high-energy training clips” or “calm, honest behind-the-scenes storytelling.” That helps a brand picture the collaboration.

5. Past Partnerships or Proof (Your Track Record)

If you’ve worked with brands — even a free product collaboration or a shoutout for a local shop — list them. Two or three logos or names build instant credibility.

No partnerships yet? Don’t fake it, and don’t panic. Substitute proof of reliability: a post you made for your school, a fundraiser you promoted, or a testimonial from a coach or club. The point is to show you can represent something well.

Brand Mistake: Padding this section with partnerships that never happened. Brands verify. One invented collaboration can cost you every future deal with that company — and word travels in NIL circles.

6. Deliverables & Ways to Work Together (What You Offer)

List the formats you can create: Instagram Reels, TikToks, story sets, a feed post, an appearance, a testimonial. This shows you understand you’re offering a service, not just existing online.

A quick note on rates: it’s fine to signal you’re open to paid, gifted, and affiliate partnerships. Keep the framing flexible and professional — you’re inviting a conversation, not posting a price list. (We’ll leave specific numbers out of your public kit; those belong in a private conversation once you understand the scope.)

7. Contact (Make It Effortless)

Your email. Maybe your best handle for DMs. That’s it. Make it impossible for an interested brand to lose track of how to reach you. You’d be amazed how many kits forget this.

Coach’s Challenge: Open your notes app right now and draft your three-sentence bio and your two-line story. Just those two things. That’s the hardest part, and once it’s done, the rest of the kit falls into place fast.

How to Present a Small (but Engaged) Audience

Let’s talk to the athlete with 900 followers, because I don’t want you to skip this section thinking you’re “too small.” You are not too small. You’re under-packaged.

Here’s a fact worth understanding. Smaller creators consistently outperform giant accounts on engagement. Industry data shows Instagram micro-influencers average around a 3.86% engagement rate, compared with roughly 1.21% for mega-influencers — and nano-influencers (under 10,000 followers) reach even higher, near 6.23% on Instagram (Archive.com, citing Sociallyin and the Captiv8 2024 Organic Benchmark Report). Always verify the current figures before you cite them — these benchmarks shift year to year.

Translation: when your audience actually knows you, trusts you, and interacts with you, a brand’s message lands harder. That’s a genuine selling point — if you present it well.

So how do you present small the right way?

  • Lead with engagement, not size. Put your engagement rate up front. “8% engagement in a tight local community” beats “just 900 followers” every time.
  • Get specific about your niche. “900 followers, almost all high school athletes and parents in my county” is a targeted audience a local brand dreams about.
  • Show relationship, not reach. Screenshot the comments where followers ask you for advice. That’s proof of influence money can’t fake.

ME3 Insight: A small, trusting audience is a feature, not an apology. Brands don’t buy followers — they buy the ability to move people. A tight community moves.

Never buy followers to look bigger. Brands can spot a bloated, low-engagement account in seconds, and it destroys the one thing your kit exists to build: trust.

Design Tips That Make You Look Legit

Your kit doesn’t need to be a graphic-design masterpiece. It needs to be clean, consistent, and easy to skim. Here’s how to get there:

  • Pick two or three colors and stick to them. Consistency reads as professionalism. Match your personal brand if you have one.
  • Use one or two fonts, max. A heading font and a body font. That’s it.
  • Give it white space. Crammed pages feel amateur. Let sections breathe.
  • Use real, high-quality photos of you. Blurry screenshots undercut everything. A few crisp action and portrait shots do a lot of heavy lifting.
  • Keep it to 1–3 pages. A brand manager with fifteen minutes will not read a ten-page kit.
  • Export as a PDF (or a shareable link). PDFs look the same on every device and feel official.

Pro Tip: If design isn’t your thing, start from a template. A good template gives you the structure and the polish, so you can focus on your words and your photos instead of fighting with margins.

The goal isn’t to look like a designer. It’s to look like someone a brand can hand their reputation to without worrying.

Common Media Kit Mistakes to Avoid

I’ve seen these over and over. Dodge them and you’re ahead of most athletes at any follower level.

  1. Leading with follower count and nothing else. Your number is context, not the whole story. Fit and engagement sell.
  2. No contact information. The interested brand shouldn’t have to hunt. Put your email where they can’t miss it.
  3. Vague audience data. “A lot of followers” tells a brand nothing. Pull the real demographics from your analytics.
  4. Outdated stats. A kit with last year’s numbers signals you’re not paying attention. Refresh it every couple of months.
  5. Inventing partnerships or inflating numbers. Brands verify. Getting caught once can close doors permanently.
  6. Ignoring the rules. NIL comes with real responsibilities — clear ad disclosures under FTC guidance, plus NCAA, state, and school-specific requirements. Your kit should reflect that you’re the kind of athlete who follows them.
  7. Making it all about you. The kit that wins quietly answers “what’s in it for the brand and their customers?”

Brand Mistake: Treating the media kit like a trophy case instead of a business proposal. The trophy case impresses your friends. The business proposal gets you rebooked.

A note on the rules, because it matters: FTC guidance requires that paid or gifted promotions be clearly disclosed, and NCAA, state laws, and individual school policies all add their own requirements — and these rules change. This article is education, not legal or financial advice. Always check the current FTC Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers and your school’s compliance office before you sign anything.

Your Action Steps

Let’s turn this into motion. Do these in order and you’ll have a real, sendable kit this week.

  1. Draft your bio and story (3–4 sentences + 1 story line). Do this first — it’s the hardest part.
  2. Pull your real analytics from Instagram and TikTok: following, engagement rate, and audience demographics.
  3. Choose your three best pieces of content to feature as examples.
  4. List your deliverables — the formats you can genuinely create well.
  5. Gather any proof — past collaborations, testimonials, or a coach’s quote.
  6. Build it on a template so the design looks polished without the stress.
  7. Export a clean PDF, add your contact email, and save it somewhere you can send in ten seconds.
  8. Set a reminder to refresh your numbers every 6–8 weeks.

Coach’s Challenge: Give yourself a deadline of one week. A finished, imperfect kit you can actually send beats a “perfect” one that never leaves your drafts.

FAQ

1. What should I include in an athlete media kit? The essentials are: your bio, your sport and a few key stats or story details, your audience size and engagement rate, audience demographics, examples of your content, any past partnerships or proof of reliability, the deliverables you offer, and your contact information. Keep it to 1–3 clean pages.

2. Do I need a big following to have a media kit? No. A media kit isn’t about size — it’s about clarity and trust. Smaller, highly engaged audiences often outperform large ones on engagement, which is exactly what many brands (especially local ones) want. Present your engagement and your niche clearly and your size becomes a strength, not a weakness.

3. How long should an athlete media kit be? One to three pages, or a short slide deck. Brand managers skim, so lead with your strongest information and cut anything that doesn’t help them make a decision. A tight kit reads as more professional than a long one.

4. Should I put my rates in my media kit? Most athletes keep specific pricing out of the public kit and open that conversation privately once they understand the scope of the deal. You can signal that you’re open to paid, gifted, and affiliate partnerships without listing numbers. Rates depend heavily on the ask, so flexibility works in your favor.

5. How often should I update my media kit? Every 6–8 weeks, or any time your numbers change meaningfully or you complete a new partnership. Outdated stats make you look inattentive; fresh numbers signal that you treat your NIL like the real opportunity it is.
