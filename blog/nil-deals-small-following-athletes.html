Case Study

You Don't Need 100K Followers: How Athletes With Small Audiences Land Real NIL Deals

By ME3 Digital·15 min read·Updated July 25, 2026

Featured Snippet Answer (40–60 words)

Do you need a big following to get an NIL deal? No. Brands increasingly pay athletes with small, engaged audiences because micro-audiences convert better and cost less. Athletes with a few thousand followers land real deals by pitching brands directly, showing professionalism, and proving they’re reliable and easy to work with — not by chasing follower count.

1. The Athlete Who Got Paid on 10,000 Followers

Julia Scoles didn’t wait to be discovered.

A beach volleyball player at USC, Scoles had around 10,600 Instagram followers — a number that would make most people assume brands weren’t calling. They weren’t. So she opened her phone and started messaging the brands she already used and loved. In the reporting on her story, she describes taking “matters into her own thumbs.”

That single decision turned into an ambassador partnership with the activewear brand Free People, plus deals with a juice company and a supplement brand. Her teammate, Hailey Harward, used the same approach — reaching out to a restaurant chain for a one-time deal and to a hydration brand she’d been drinking for years, which turned into regular product and features.

No agent. No viral moment. No 100,000 followers. Just two athletes who treated their small audience like a business and asked.

The lesson generalizes: athletes who aren’t being recruited by brands can recruit brands themselves.

Here’s what most athletes miss about that story. Scoles didn’t win because she was famous. She won because she was proactive, targeted, and professional — she pitched brands that actually fit her, she made it easy to say yes, and she followed through. That combination is available to you at 800 followers or 8,000. This article breaks down exactly how it works, and how to do it yourself.

ME3 Insight: At our agency, when we build a brand campaign, we’re not just buying reach — we’re buying reliability. A 10,000-follower athlete who responds fast, delivers clean content on time, and understands disclosure is worth more to us than a 90,000-follower athlete we have to chase. Follower count gets you in the room. Professionalism gets you rehired.

2. Why Brands Are Quietly Choosing Smaller Athletes

For a few years, NIL headlines were all about the seven-figure quarterbacks and million-follower gymnasts. That’s real — but it’s the top 1%, and it’s not where most deals actually live.

The bigger, quieter shift is that brands have moved money toward smaller creators, including athletes. There are three practical reasons.

Smaller audiences trust the athlete more

When someone with a few thousand followers recommends a protein bar, it reads like a friend’s recommendation. When a celebrity with two million followers does it, it reads like an ad. Brands know this, and they’re paying for the trust — not just the eyeballs.

Smaller deals are easier to say yes to

A local nutrition shop, a regional apparel line, or a growing app can’t afford a national star. They can afford five athletes in their target market. For those brands, an engaged local athlete isn’t a compromise — it’s the entire strategy.

Athletes can be bundled

One of the smartest moves happening right now is group deals. In one example, five Florida college soccer players combined their audiences — more than 56,000 followers together — to sign with a regional fitness apparel brand. Individually, none of them may have been “enough.” Together, they were exactly the local reach the brand wanted.

Pro Tip: If your following feels too small to pitch alone, don’t shrink your ambition — pool it. Three or four teammates with aligned audiences can offer a brand something no single one of you could.

3. What the Numbers Actually Say About Engagement

Follower count is a vanity metric. Engagement is the one brands actually pay for — and this is where smaller athletes have a structural advantage.

Recent 2026 influencer-marketing data shows a clear pattern: the smaller the audience, the higher the average engagement rate.

Audience Tier Typical Instagram Engagement Rate
Nano (under 10K followers) ~6.2%
Micro (10K–50K followers) ~3.9%
Mega (1.5M+ followers) ~1.2%–2.8%

In plain terms: micro-influencers deliver roughly three times the engagement of mega-influencers relative to their audience size. That’s not a rounding error — that’s the whole reason a brand chooses you.

And brands have noticed. Industry surveys in 2026 show the majority now prefer partnering with micro and mid-tier creators over celebrities, and nano-influencers are the single most preferred tier for many brands.

A small, engaged audience isn’t a consolation prize. To the right brand, it’s the product.

So when you look at your own account and think “I don’t have enough,” reframe it. The question a brand is really asking isn’t “How many people follow you?” It’s “How many people actually listen to you — and will they act?”

ME3 Insight: We’ve seen brands pass on athletes with big numbers and dead comment sections, and green-light athletes with a fraction of the following and a community that shows up. When we evaluate a creator, an engaged 4,000 beats a hollow 40,000 almost every time.

4. The 5-Part Playbook Small-Following Athletes Use

Here’s the system behind stories like Scoles’ and Harward’s. None of it requires going viral. All of it requires acting like a professional.

Step 1 — Target brands you already use

The most believable partnership is one that’s already true. List the brands actually in your life: the drink in your bag, the shoes you train in, the local smoothie spot, the recovery tool you swear by. Authentic fit is what made Scoles’ and Harward’s outreach work — they pitched brands they genuinely supported, so the ask made sense.

Step 2 — Go local and regional first

National brands get thousands of pitches. The nutrition shop two miles from campus gets almost none — and they’d love an athlete their customers recognize. Local and regional businesses are the most realistic first “yes” for a small-following athlete.

Step 3 — Lead with value, not follower count

Don’t open with your numbers. Open with what you can do for them: reach a specific local audience, create authentic content, show up to an event, represent them reliably. A simple, professional media kit that frames your engagement and your fit does more than a screenshot of your follower total.

Step 4 — Pitch directly and professionally

This is the step most athletes skip. Send the DM. Send the email. Keep it short, specific, and respectful of their time: who you are, why you fit their brand, and one clear idea. You are not begging — you’re offering a partnership. Professional outreach is a learnable skill, and it’s the single highest-leverage thing a small-following athlete can do.

Step 5 — Deliver like a pro, then follow up

The first deal is a tryout. Hit your deadlines, disclose properly, over-communicate, and deliver content the brand is proud to post. Then follow up. Harward’s hydration deal didn’t stay a one-time thing — it became ongoing because she kept showing up. Repeat business is where NIL income actually compounds.

Coach’s Challenge: This week, write down five brands you already use and genuinely love — three of them local. Those five names are your first pitch list. You don’t need a bigger audience to start. You need to start.

5. Expert Insight: What We Look For Before Follower Count

At ME3 Digital, we’ve spent years on the brand side of NIL — including helping execute Hooters’ national offensive-line NIL campaign that spanned more than 50 student-athletes from 17 universities and earned Brand of the Year at the 2023 NIL Awards. That means we’ve sat in the room where athletes get chosen. And we can tell you plainly: follower count is rarely the first thing we discuss.

Here’s what actually moves an athlete up our list, often before we ever look at their audience size:

Do they make us feel safe? Brands are handing you their reputation. A clean, consistent, on-brand social presence signals you won’t become a liability.

Are they easy to work with? Fast replies. Clear communication. No chasing. An athlete who responds within a day and asks smart questions is a joy — and gets rehired.

Can they actually create? One good, authentic piece of content is worth more than a huge audience that scrolls past. We’re buying content that performs, not just a place to post it.

Do they understand the rules? An athlete who knows they need to disclose a paid partnership — and does it correctly — tells us they’re a professional. One who doesn’t is a risk we may not take.

Most NIL advice teaches athletes how to promote themselves. The athletes who win learn how to become genuinely valuable, credible, and easy for a brand to say yes to.

That’s the reframe. You don’t need to out-follow anyone. You need to out-professional them.

6. Common Mistakes That Cost Small-Following Athletes Deals

We see the same avoidable errors sink promising athletes. Watch for these.

Waiting to be discovered. The biggest one. Brands are not scrolling for you. The athletes who get paid reach out first.

Leading with an apology. “I only have 3,000 followers, but…” You just told the brand to focus on the one number you’re weakest on. Lead with fit and value instead.

Pitching brands that make no sense. Asking a luxury car company to sponsor a high schooler wastes everyone’s time. Relevance beats reach — pitch brands your audience would actually believe you use.

Brand Mistake: Copy-pasting the same generic DM to 50 brands. Brands can smell a mass message instantly, and it reads as “I want money,” not “I want to help you.” Five personalized pitches beat fifty copy-pastes every time.

Going quiet after the first “yes.” Missing deadlines or disappearing after signing is how a one-time deal stays a one-time deal. Your delivery is your audition for the next one.

Skipping disclosure. If a partnership is paid or you got free product, you’re required to disclose it clearly (think #ad or #sponsored) under FTC guidelines. It’s not optional, and rules can also vary by your state, school, and conference. Getting this right protects you and signals professionalism to the brand.

NIL Brand Academy provides education, not legal advice. Always confirm the current rules with your school’s compliance office, since state, school, and NCAA policies change and differ.

7. Your Action Steps This Week

You can start today. No new followers required.

  1. Build your pitch list. Write down 5–10 brands you already use, at least half of them local or regional.
  2. Audit your profile. Make your bio clear, your recent posts on-brand, and your contact info visible. Assume a brand will look.
  3. Draft one professional pitch. Short, specific, and about them — who you are, why you fit their brand, one content idea.
  4. Prepare a simple media kit. One page: your sport, your audience, your engagement, your content style, and how to reach you.
  5. Send three pitches this week. Not thirty. Three good, personalized ones. Track who you contacted and when.
  6. Set a follow-up reminder. If you don’t hear back in 5–7 days, send one polite follow-up. Persistence is professional.

Coach’s Challenge: Do not close this article and “think about it.” Send one pitch before the end of the day. The gap between athletes who get NIL deals and those who don’t usually isn’t talent or followers — it’s who actually hit send.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get an NIL deal with under 1,000 followers? Yes. Especially with local businesses and group deals, athletes with very small audiences can land partnerships — often product, event, or small-payment deals to start. High engagement and professionalism matter more than a follower threshold.

How many followers do you need for an NIL deal? There is no minimum. Brands care about engagement, authenticity, fit, and reliability far more than a specific follower count. Many deals go to athletes in the nano (under 10K) and micro (10K–50K) tiers.

What’s the fastest way for a small-following athlete to get a deal? Pitch brands you already use, start local, lead with value instead of follower count, and reach out directly with a short, professional message. Proactive outreach is the highest-leverage move you can make.

Do brands really prefer smaller athletes? Many do for certain campaigns. Smaller, engaged audiences tend to trust the athlete more and cost less, and 2026 data shows the majority of brands now favor micro and mid-tier creators over celebrities for a large share of their partnerships.

Do I have to disclose a small or product-only NIL deal? Yes. If you received payment or free product in exchange for a post, FTC guidelines require a clear disclosure (like #ad or #sponsored). Also check your school and state rules, which can vary.

9. Conclusion: Small Audience, Serious Business

Somewhere right now, an athlete with 40,000 followers is waiting for a brand to notice them. And an athlete with 4,000 is drafting a pitch.

Guess which one gets paid first.

The NIL money isn’t reserved for the famous. It flows to the athletes who treat their audience — however small — like a business, who understand that brands buy trust and reliability, and who are willing to send the message everyone else is too nervous to send. Julia Scoles proved it on around 10,000 followers. You can start proving it on far fewer.

Your following is your launch pad, not your ceiling. Now go pitch like the professional you’re becoming.

You don’t need a bigger audience to get brand ready. You need a plan, a pitch, and the nerve to hit send.
