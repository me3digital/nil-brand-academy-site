Getting Started

How to Get Your First NIL Deal: A Step-by-Step Guide for Student-Athletes

By ME3 Digital·18 min read·Updated July 25, 2026

Featured Snippet Answer: To get your first NIL deal, get “brand-ready” by cleaning up your profiles, then build two assets: an athlete profile and a media kit. Identify local businesses and brands that authentically fit you, craft a short professional pitch, and reach out by email or DM. Deliver reliably, disclose the partnership, and follow up to earn repeat deals.

The DM That Actually Got Answered

Picture two athletes on the same team, same sport, same city. One has 40,000 followers. The other has 900.

The one with 40,000 sends a brand a message that says: “Hey, I’ll post for you. What can you offer?”

The one with 900 sends the local smoothie shop a two-line email: “I train four mornings a week and I’m in your shop after every session. I’d love to show my followers why. Here’s a quick media kit with my audience and three post ideas.”

Guess who got the reply.

Here’s the thing nobody tells you when you start googling how to get NIL deals: brands are not shopping for followers. They’re shopping for a partner who won’t make their job harder. That’s the entire game, and it’s a game you can win right now — today — no matter how big your following is.

ME3 Insight: Follower count creates opportunity. Professionalism creates repeat opportunity. One deal is luck. Ten deals is a reputation.

Let’s build that reputation together. This is the exact step-by-step system I’d walk you through if you were sitting across from me. By the end, you’ll know how to get your first NIL deal — and how to set up the next one before the first one even ends.

First, a Quick Reality Check on the Rules

Before we pitch a single brand, you need to know the guardrails — because they protect you.

NIL stands for Name, Image, and Likeness. Since the NCAA adopted its interim NIL policy in 2021, college athletes have been allowed to earn money from their personal brand (NCAA). The landscape shifted again when a judge granted final approval to the House v. NCAA settlement in 2025, opening the door to direct athlete compensation and new rules that schools are still rolling out (ESPN).

For high school athletes, it depends entirely on where you live. As of 2025, roughly 40 states plus Washington, D.C. permit some form of high school NIL activity, while others prohibit it or are still deciding (Sports Illustrated; On3 tracker).

And no matter your level, one rule is universal: if a brand gives you money, free product, or a discount in exchange for a post, the Federal Trade Commission requires you to clearly disclose it — with a hard-to-miss label like “#ad” or “sponsored,” right on the post itself (FTC).

This is education, not legal or financial advice. NIL rules vary by state, school, and sport — and they change fast. Always check with your compliance office, athletic department, or a qualified advisor before signing anything.

Rules handled. Now let’s go get you a deal.

Step 1: Get Brand-Ready (Before You Pitch Anyone)

Here’s a scenario. A local orthodontist is deciding between two athletes to sponsor. They open the first athlete’s Instagram and the top post is a blurry gym mirror pic with a caption full of shorthand no one outside the locker room understands. They open the second athlete’s profile: a clear bio that says who they are, what they play, and where — plus a highlight reel and a way to reach them.

The orthodontist never even makes it to the DMs of the first athlete. You lost the deal before you knew it existed.

Getting brand-ready means auditing your digital presence through a brand’s eyes, not yours. Ask:

  • Is my bio clear? Sport, school/level, city, and a contact method. A brand should understand you in three seconds.
  • Is my content safe? Scrub anything you wouldn’t want a sponsor — or a college coach — to see. Brands run background checks with their eyes.
  • Am I consistent? You don’t need to post daily. You need to look like someone who shows up.

Pro Tip: Switch your Instagram or TikTok to a professional/creator account. It unlocks insights (your reach, audience age, and location) — the exact numbers a brand will ask for.

You’re not trying to look like a celebrity. You’re trying to look like a reliable partner. That’s a much lower bar and a much stronger position.

Step 2: Build Your Two Core Assets

Every professional athlete-partner walks into a conversation with two things ready. Build these once, update them forever.

Asset 1: Your Athlete Profile

This is your one-paragraph story. Who you are, what you play, what you stand for, and who your audience is. It’s the foundation of every pitch. Think of it as your personal brand in a single, confident breath.

Asset 2: Your Media Kit

This is the asset that separates amateurs from pros. A media kit is a clean one- or two-page document that gives a brand everything they need to say “yes”: your photo, sport and level, follower counts and engagement rate, audience demographics, and a few sample content ideas.

Why does it matter so much? Because it does the brand’s homework for them. A marketing manager juggling 30 tasks doesn’t want to dig through your profile to figure out if you’re a fit. Hand them a media kit and you’ve just become the easiest athlete they’ll talk to all week.

ME3 Insight: A media kit isn’t bragging — it’s service. You’re removing friction from a busy person’s day. The athlete who’s easiest to say yes to usually is.

Step 3: Identify the Right Brands (Start Local, Start Authentic)

New athletes make the same mistake: they aim straight for the biggest national brand they can think of. Nike isn’t answering your DM this week. But the smoothie shop by campus? The physical therapy clinic your teammates use? The family-owned sporting goods store? Those are real, reachable, and often eager to support a local athlete.

Build a list of 15–20 targets using three filters:

  1. Proximity — local businesses in your town or near your school.
  2. Authenticity — brands you genuinely use or believe in. If you don’t drink energy drinks, don’t pitch one.
  3. Fit — brands whose customers look like your audience.

The authenticity filter isn’t just about ethics — it’s about psychology. Your followers can smell a fake endorsement instantly, and so can the brand. When you promote something you actually use, the content is better, the audience trusts it, and the brand gets a real result. Everyone wins.

Brand Mistake: Chasing a brand that has nothing to do with your life. A single authentic local partnership beats ten random pitches to companies that will never see themselves in you.

Not sure a small following can land these? It can — and often converts better because your audience is tight and trusting. See our case study on athletes with small followings.

Step 4: Craft the Pitch

Your pitch is not “will you sponsor me?” That question puts all the work on the brand. Your pitch answers a different question: “Here’s exactly how I can help you.”

A strong first-contact pitch has four parts:

  1. A genuine hook — one specific line showing you actually know their business.
  2. Who you are — your athlete profile in one sentence.
  3. The value — what you can create for them (a reel, a game-day shoutout, an appearance).
  4. A soft ask — an invitation to talk, not a demand.

Keep it short. Three to five sentences. A busy owner should be able to read it at a red light.

Pro Tip: Lead with them, not you. “I love that your shop sources local beans” beats “I have 3,000 followers” every single time. Make them feel seen before you ask to be seen.

Writing these from a blank page is the hardest part — which is exactly why we built plug-and-play templates for it (more on that below).

Step 5: Reach Out Professionally

You’ve got your list and your pitch. Now, contact.

Email beats DM when you can find one. An email to the owner or marketing contact reads as more serious and is easier for them to forward internally. Use a clean subject line: “Local athlete partnership idea — [Your Name].” Attach your media kit.

If you only have a DM, keep it just as professional. No slang, no wall of text, no “u.” Introduce yourself, share your one-line pitch, and offer to send your media kit.

Then — this is the part most athletes skip — track it. Keep a simple spreadsheet: brand, contact, date you reached out, and response. This is how you turn “I sent some messages” into an actual outreach system.

Coach’s Challenge: Send five real, personalized pitches this week. Not fifty copy-pastes — five that each name something specific about the brand. Five thoughtful shots beat fifty blanks.

Expect silence sometimes. That’s not rejection; it’s normal. Professionals follow up (more on that in Step 7).

Step 6: Deliver Like a Pro

You got a yes. Congratulations — now the real opportunity begins, because how you deliver on deal one determines whether there’s a deal two.

Delivering like a pro means:

  • Hit the deadline. If they need it by Friday, it’s live Thursday.
  • Follow the brief. If they asked you to tag the account and use a specific hashtag, do exactly that.
  • Disclose the partnership. Always add “#ad” or “sponsored” clearly on the post — the FTC requires it, and it signals you’re someone who does things right (FTC). For the full breakdown, see our NIL Disclosure & FTC Rules guide.
  • Send proof. After it posts, send the brand a quick note with a screenshot and early numbers.

That last step is quietly powerful. Most athletes go silent after posting. When you proactively report results, you’re doing the brand’s reporting for them — again, removing friction.

ME3 Insight: Anyone can post. The pro sends the screenshot, the numbers, and a thank-you. That thirty-second follow-through is the difference between a one-off and a partner.

Step 7: Follow Up for Repeat Deals

Here’s the secret the follower-chasers never learn: the easiest deal to get is the second one with a brand that already loves working with you.

After you deliver, keep the relationship warm:

  • Send a thank-you. Simple, genuine, human.
  • Share the results. “That post drove 40 profile visits to your page — happy to do a follow-up for game day.”
  • Pitch the next idea. You’ve already earned trust. Use it.

And when a first pitch goes unanswered? A single polite follow-up a week later lands deals constantly. People are busy, not uninterested. One nudge shows you’re serious.

Coach’s Challenge: For every deal you complete, pitch one new idea to that same brand within two weeks. That’s how one deal quietly becomes a season of them.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Waiting until you’re “big enough.” There’s no magic number. Local brands work with local athletes today. Start now.
  • Copy-paste spray pitches. A generic “will you sponsor me?” to 50 brands loses to five personalized notes. Every time.
  • Ignoring the rules. Skipping FTC disclosure or your school’s compliance process can cost you eligibility and trust. Check first (FTC).
  • No media kit. Making a brand dig for your info is friction. Friction loses deals.
  • Going silent after posting. No proof, no thank-you, no next idea = no second deal.
  • Chasing money over fit. A mismatched brand produces flat content and a partnership neither side wants to repeat.

Your Action Steps

Do these in order this week:

  1. Audit your profiles. Clean bio, safe content, professional account switched on.
  2. Write your athlete profile. One confident paragraph.
  3. Build your media kit. One page: photo, stats, audience, sample ideas.
  4. List 15–20 target brands. Local, authentic, on-fit.
  5. Personalize five pitches. Name something specific about each brand.
  6. Send them — email first, DM if needed. Track every one in a spreadsheet.
  7. Follow up in a week. One polite nudge.
  8. When you land one, over-deliver and disclose. Then pitch idea number two.

FAQ

1. How do athletes get NIL deals with a small following? By being professional and authentic, not famous. Small, local audiences often trust and convert better. A clear media kit, an on-fit brand, and a personalized pitch beat a big-but-generic account. See our small-following case study.

2. Can high school athletes get NIL deals? In many states, yes. As of 2025, roughly 40 states plus D.C. permit some high school NIL activity, but others prohibit it — it depends entirely on your state and school (Sports Illustrated). Always confirm your local rules first.

3. What should my first NIL pitch actually say? Four short parts: a genuine hook about their business, one sentence on who you are, the specific value you’ll create, and a soft invitation to talk. Keep it to three to five sentences and attach your media kit.

4. Do I really have to disclose sponsored posts? Yes. The FTC requires a clear, hard-to-miss disclosure like “#ad” or “sponsored” on the post itself whenever you receive money, product, or a discount (FTC). It also builds trust with your audience.

5. How long until I land my first deal? There’s no guaranteed timeline — it varies by sport, location, and effort. What you can control is your process: brand-ready profiles, a strong media kit, and consistent, personalized outreach. Focus on reps, not results.
